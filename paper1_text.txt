ABSTRACT We present a compact CMOS leaky integrate-and-fire neuron that operates natively in two
regimes: subthreshold for ultra-low power and saturation for robust interfacing. This dual-mode functionality
bridges the energy efficiency of the subthreshold operation with the connectivity advantage of saturation
within a single circuit. The design leverages device-level parasitics and subthreshold conduction to minimize
the energy consumption per spike and reduce silicon area. It also maintains sufficient headroom in the
saturation region to drive or directly interface with non-subthreshold components and resistive memories.
In addition to outlining the design methodology and sizing strategy that enable dual-mode operation
without needing additional amplification stages, we introduce a mathematical model that captures the
circuit dynamics. Under specified operating assumptions, this model allows us to predict the spiking
frequency for various application targets. SPICE simulations validate the expected spiking dynamics and
trade-offs between energy consumption and latency. We also compare our proposed neuron with recent
CMOS implementations reported in the literature. While some earlier designs achieve lower absolute energy
consumption, our approach features a significantly reduced transistor count and the ability to interface
with other cells without dedicated amplification, offering a practical and integration-friendly solution.
By converting unavoidable capacitive parasitics into advantages, this work presents a silicon-efficient
method for low-power neuromorphic hardware, making both subthreshold efficiency and saturation-range
connectivity key design objectives.

theme in the design of neuron circuits for neuromorphic

This work presents a compact analog architecture for a
leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) spiking neuron model. The
proposed model uses a mixed subthreshold and saturation
operation, combining the benefits of both approaches. It takes
advantage of the low energy consumption per spike event,
which is characteristic of subthreshold operation while
maintaining the rail-to-rail output swing and, therefore,
the level compatibility offered by saturation. The main
concept is to utilize a single subthreshold-operated device
for charge and leak integration while maintaining the other
stages in saturation for functions such as thresholding,
spiking, resetting, and input/output operations. This approach
enables a functional implementation with fewer transis-
tors, eliminating the need for additional coupling stages
or multiple supply rails. Avoiding these stages reduces
typical area and power overheads, facilitating plug-and-play
integration with saturation-range analog and digital blocks,

B. SUBTHRESHOLD CONDUCTION
In digital electronics, MOS transistors are commonly oper-
ated in strong inversion to toggle between the logical states
of cut-off and saturation. However, describing a device as
‘off’ is a simplifying abstraction that doesn’t reflect the
underlying physics of the device. From a physical standpoint,
even when the gate-to-source voltage is below threshold,
the transistor conducts a small but finite current [10],
[11], [12], governed by carrier diffusion along the channel
rather than by the drift-dominated transport characteristic of
strong inversion. While drift-current mechanisms in MOS
transistors are extensively treated in the literature [13],

[14], the diffusion-driven conduction that dominates in
the subthreshold regime is often given comparatively less
emphasis in circuit-level expositions.
When a MOS transistor operates under strong electric
fields, such as in the case of strong inversion, both electrons
(the negative carriers in N-MOS transistors) and holes
(the positive carriers in P-MOS transistors) are primarily
influenced by the applied field between the drain and source
terminals. Electrons drift toward the positive drain terminal,
while holes drift toward the negative source terminal. This
movement, driven by the electric field, is referred to as
drift current (Fig. 3). By contrast, diffusion current arises
from carrier concentration gradients. Regions with higher
electron/hole concentrations tend to spread into regions of
lower concentration, producing a net current even in the
absence of a strong external electric field (Fig. 4).

III. CIRCUIT DESIGN AND OPERATION
This section describes the proposed circuit and its operation
across the functional stages, highlighting the key expressions
and parameters that enable estimation of the firing frequency.
A. CIRCUIT OPERATION
The proposed circuit (Fig. 5) is organized into three main
stages: integration and reset (transistor M1, operated in
subthreshold), threshold detection and spike generation
(transistors M2–M3), and gain with feedback-based reset
(transistors M4–M5). Next, we will explain each operating
stage in detail.
1) Integration and reset (Fig. 6(a)): The excitation
current Iex charges the membrane capacitance at the
drain node of transistor M1, which is formed by
the aggregate of intrinsic and parasitic capacitances
connected to that node. As the membrane voltage Vm
increases and reaches the inverter trip point Vth(lif),
which defines the membrane threshold and is set by
transistors M2–M3, the subsequent stage of operation
is activated.
2) Threshold and spike (Fig. 6(b)): The M2–M3 inverter
senses Vm and toggles abruptly at its switching
threshold Vth(lif), producing the spike that drives the
following stage.
3) Gain and feedback reset (Fig. 6(c)): The M4–M5
inverter stage provides the final output and feeds back
a short reset pulse. Upon receiving this feedback,
M1 is momentarily driven into conduction, enabling
the leakage path and discharging Cm. Once the pulse
ends, M1 returns to its subthreshold integration role,
starting a new charge cycle and thus yielding a periodic
spike train. In addition, the neuron’s output voltage,
Vout, reaches digital-compatible levels, up to 1.8 V for
SKY130 nominal transistors (Fig. 7(c)).
This feedback mechanism yields stable oscillatory behav-
ior without the need for external passive components, relying
solely on the device’s intrinsic capacitances.
B. LIF CIRCUIT MODEL
The proposed circuit consists of five transistors and an
excitation current source, as shown in Fig. 5. Transistor M1
performs the integration of the input excitation current Iex,
feed at the neuron input. It exploits the parasitic capacitances
present at its drain node, together with its intrinsic channel
resistance in the subthreshold region, to establish an effective
membrane time constant τ = RleakCm, as long as the
membrane voltage Vm remains below the inverter trip
point Vth(lif). Once the gate voltage exceeds this threshold,
M1 transitions to a strong-conduction regime and rapidly
discharges the accumulated charge at the membrane node,
thereby implementing the reset operation (see Fig. 6(a)).
To quantify the leakage resistance associated with the Vm
node in the subthreshold regime, denoted as Rleak, it is

controlled-leaky element, providing a well-defined, tunable
effective resistance that governs the membrane dynamics
prior to threshold crossing.
Additionally, the gate signal switches rapidly between
voltage rails. This transition must happen quickly; as soon

the integration currents are in the range of nanoamperes, Rleak

This transient negative-voltage behavior at the Cm node
arises from charge redistribution effects, primarily driven by
the gate-to-drain parasitic capacitance of M1 during the fast
transition of the Vgs1 pulse. When the reset signal switches
abruptly, capacitive coupling between the gate and drain
momentarily injects charge into the membrane node, pulling
the Vm voltage below ground. This effect is exacerbated when
the effective membrane capacitance is dominated by small
parasitic components. By increasing Cm through the addition
of a small explicit MiM capacitor, the relative impact of this
parasitic charge injection is significantly reduced, and the
transient negative excursion at the membrane node effectively
vanishes. In this case, the larger total capacitance absorbs the
injected charge, preventing appreciable voltage undershoot.
When a transient negative voltage appears at the drain of M1,
the drain-substrate p-n junction may become forward-biased.
Since this junction begins to conduct at approximately 0.7 V,
corresponding to the typical forward voltage of a silicon
diode, the maximum negative excursion of the drain voltage is
inherently limited to approximately −Vf ≈ −0.7 V. Forward
conduction clamps the membrane potential and prevents
further decrease.
The leakage resistance at the drain of M1, denoted as
Rleak, varies as a function of the membrane voltage. When
the membrane voltage Vm approaches zero, the effective
resistance is relatively low. As Vm increases during the
integration phase, Rleak correspondingly increases. When
the membrane potential reaches the firing threshold, the
operating regime of M1 changes abruptly: the device exits
the subthreshold region and rapidly transitions into strong
inversion. This regime change enables a much larger drain-
source current, which efficiently discharges the charge
accumulated at the Vm node, thereby resetting the membrane

ultimately set by the inverter switching threshold, which
defines the neuron firing level and marks the transition to the
second stage of circuit operation.

Equation (16) provides the inverter switching voltage,

This implies that the threshold can be tuned to tailor the
neuron’s behavior. At first glance, one might expect a lower
threshold to accelerate firing, whereas a higher threshold
would delay it.
However, because transistor M1 operates in the subthresh-
old region, its behavior is highly sensitive to the parasitic
capacitances present at the membrane node. As a result,
any modification in the sizing of transistors M2 and M3
(i.e., their W /L ratios) alters not only the inverter switching
threshold but also the effective capacitive loading seen at
the membrane node, directly affecting the integration time
constant. Consequently, increasing the inverter switching
threshold Vth(lif) alone does not necessarily lead to a longer
integration interval or increased firing latency. Instead, the
neuron dynamics are primarily governed by the effective
membrane time constant τ = RleakCm, which depends on
both the subthreshold leakage resistance of M1 and the total
capacitance at the membrane node.
In practice, Vth(lif) must therefore be co-designed with
the membrane capacitance, comprising parasitic and optional
explicit components, and with the subthreshold leakage
characteristics that define Rleak. This co-design approach
enables controlled tuning of the firing frequency through
time-constant engineering rather than relying solely on
threshold adjustment.
D. PARASITIC CAPACITANCE CONSIDERATIONS
To complete the analysis, it is necessary to determine the
value of Cm, which represents the equivalent capacitance at
the integration (membrane) node. This capacitance is formed
by the cumulative parasitic contributions of all transistors
connected to the node, as well as by any external elements
responsible for delivering the excitation current. It is impor-
tant to emphasize that, when operating in the subthreshold
region, transistor M1 becomes highly sensitive to capacitive
effects at the membrane node. While subthreshold operation
offers substantial benefits, most notably, a significant reduc-
tion in energy consumption, it also increases susceptibility to
process variations, parasitic loading, and noise coupling. As a
result, an accurate estimation of Cm is essential for predicting
the neuron’s temporal dynamics and firing behavior.
Considering only transistors M1, M2, and M3, the total
membrane capacitance can be approximated as:
Ct = CgdM1 + CbdM1 + CgsM2 + CgdM2 + CgbM2
+ CgsM3 + CgdM3 + CgbM3 + Cm, (17)
where Ct denotes any explicit capacitor connected at the node.
It follows that larger transistor sizes lead to larger parasitic
capacitances; therefore, a balance between device sizing and
parasitics must be established to achieve the desired circuit
behavior.
A practical issue arises when an ideal current source is
connected directly to the integration node, as illustrated in
Fig. 9, since such a source does not contribute any parasitic
capacitance. Although this idealization may be acceptable for
a first-order analysis, the excitation currents of interest lie in
the nanoampere range. Under these conditions, even small
variations in the effective capacitance at the membrane node
can lead to noticeable changes in the charging dynamics and,
consequently, in the neuron firing frequency, as illustrated in
Fig. 10.

At this stage, the design can follow one of two
complementary strategies:
1) Introduce a small additional capacitance as an explicit
design parameter to stabilize the integration node.
2) Isolate the integration node using a dedicated input
element to decouple it from external parasitic
effects.
In this work, we deliberately combine both strategies
because they address different (and complementary) sources
of variability at the integration node. Adding a small explicit
Cm provides a well-defined capacitive reference that reduces
the relative impact of parasitic charge injection (e.g., via
gate–drain coupling during fast switching) and stabilizes the
membrane trajectory, preventing undesired voltage under-
shoot. In parallel, introducing a dedicated input (isolation)
element decouples the membrane node from the excitation
source and its associated parasitics, ensuring that variations in
the current-delivery circuitry do not unintentionally modulate
the effective node capacitance.
Therefore, by combining isolation and explicit capaci-
tance, we turn the membrane dynamics into a controlled,
designable time constant τ = RleakCm, improving the
predictability of the firing frequency, robustness against
parasitic fluctuations, and compatibility with integration in
larger mixed-signal systems.

V. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
Finally, in this section, the main findings are synthesized
and potential improvements aimed at specific applications are
presented.
A. CONCLUSIONS
This work presents the design and analysis of a compact,
dual-mode CMOS LIF neuron that leverages a single
subthreshold-operated device for ultra-low-power integra-
tion, combined with saturation-region transistors that provide
robust thresholding, reset, and full-swing input/output opera-
tion. The proposed architecture achieves low energy per spike
while preserving a practical output voltage swing, enabling
direct interfacing with saturation-range analog and digital
blocks without the need for external amplification stages or
multiple supply rails.
Compared with neuron circuits implemented purely in
saturation, the proposed circuit reduces energy consumption
for low-frequency workloads by exploiting subthreshold
conduction and the membrane node’s equivalent parasitic
capacitance, all with a very low transistor count (5T baseline,
6T with isolation).
Relative to state-of-the-art subthreshold-only LIF designs,
our neuron maintains a similarly small device budget while
slightly increasing consumption to gain two pragmatic
advantages: (i) elimination of dedicated output amplifiers
or level shifters, and (ii) single-supply operation, which
substantially simplifies system-level integration. These char-
acteristics make the proposed neuron both novel and

B. FUTURE WORK
Several directions can further improve robustness and
tunability:
• Digitally controlled capacitor bank: add an on-chip
Cm bank with simple digital control to trim the firing
frequency post-fabrication.
• Control and extensibility: because the neuron’s input
and output stages operate in saturation, it is straightfor-
ward to incorporate control features and mixed-signal
hooks. For example, an inhibitory gate (a transistor that
momentarily blocks current into the integrate node) can
be added to suppress spiking; multiple input transistors
can be placed in parallel to accept several excitation sig-
nals; and, more generally, the saturation-mode interface
simplifies the integration of additional digital or analog
circuitry as needed.
